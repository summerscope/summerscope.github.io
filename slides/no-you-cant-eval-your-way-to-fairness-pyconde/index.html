before we measure, we need to agree on what we're measuring.
section three
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'We have felt what unfairness feels like. Now I want to give you some vocabulary — not to replace that feeling, but to give it some structure. Two categories of harm. Both matter. They are not the same thing.'
1

harms of allocation

when a system withholds or unfairly distributes opportunities, resources or penalties.

2

harms of representation

when a system reinforces the subordination of a group through how it depicts or describes people.

Barocas, Crawford, Shapiro & Wallach, 2017 · Crawford, NeurIPS 2017

Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'These two categories come from Kate Crawford's 2017 NeurIPS keynote, The Trouble with Bias, and a paper by Barocas, Crawford, Shapiro and Wallach from the same year. Allocative harms are about what you get or don't get — a loan, a job, a shorter sentence, a higher insurance premium. Immediate, legible, and the ones most fairness tooling tries to measure. Representational harms are subtler and longer-term — about how people are seen, or not seen, in data and outputs. They shape what is considered normal, who is considered capable, who is considered to exist at all. And they sit at the root of most allocative harms downstream.'

harms of allocation

1

low risk

an automated rewards system at a doctor's surgery. algorithm de-prioritises the treat for patients outside the target age range. a child doesn't get a candy. upsetting but recoverable.

2

high risk

COMPAS. twice as likely to falsely flag Black defendants as future offenders. people stayed in prison longer based on a score nobody could explain. the time does not come back. (Angwin et al., ProPublica 2016)

Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Same structure, allocation side. Low stakes: an automated patient rewards system that de-prioritises the treat for patients outside the target age range. A child doesn't get a candy. Real harm, small, recoverable. High stakes: COMPAS. A risk assessment algorithm used in US courts to predict likelihood of reoffending. ProPublica's 2016 investigation found it was twice as likely to falsely flag Black defendants as future offenders compared to white defendants. Designed as a decision aid. In practice, judges deferred to it. People stayed in prison longer, or were denied bail, based on a score nobody in the courtroom could fully explain. The harm is not recoverable. The time does not come back. The point connecting all four examples: the same habits of mind that produce the broken filter are the same habits that, at higher stakes, produce COMPAS.'

harms of representation

1

low risk

a face filter that puts bunny ears on you. cute if your face is detected. the effect breaks for darker skin tones. annoying. a minor indignity. nobody's life is at stake.

2

high risk

a teenage girl searches for mathematicians and sees almost entirely white men. she updates her sense of what's possible. she changes course. the erasure compounds.

Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Two examples of representational harm, very different stakes. A Snapchat-style filter trained predominantly on lighter skin tones — for users with darker skin the ears sit wrong, the effect breaks. Annoying. A minor indignity. But the system is saying quietly: we didn't really think about you when we built this. Now the high-stakes version. A teenage girl, good at maths, trying to work out what she could become. She searches for mathematicians, scientists, engineers. Almost entirely white men. She updates her sense of what's possible. She changes course. This is the Hidden Figures problem — not just that Katherine Johnson was overlooked in her lifetime, but that the erasure compounds. Every generation of girls who can't see themselves in the field is a generation less likely to enter it, which means less representation in the next generation's data. Representational harm is not just a reflection of inequality. It is a mechanism for reproducing it.'

understand your ingredients

what's baked into the foundation models and systems you build on.

intervene

harm reduction, relative risk assessment, feedback loops, monitoring, positive interventions.

Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Four jobs. Jobs one, two and three are structurally identical — build intuition, then measure — at three different levels of abstraction. Job one is the world. Job two is your ingredients. Job three is your specific system. Most people only ever do job three, and usually only after the system is already in production. Jobs one and two are almost always skipped. Job four — intervention — is almost always done badly or not at all. That is what we are building toward. And one more thing: any attempt to do this work without intersectionality will systematically miss the most marginalised people. Intersectionality is not optional. It is the difference between measuring the average and measuring the people who are most at risk.'
qualitative + quantitative

a metric is only as good as the hypothesis behind it

  • study the domain — read, follow your curiosity
  • talk to people affected by the system
  • use scanning tools and design activities
  • then measure what you've learned to look for
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'When I say measure harm I do not only mean run a library against your outputs. Quantitative and qualitative are complements, not alternatives. The library does not tell you what to look for. Domain knowledge does. Reading the literature does. Talking to people whose lives the system touches does. Design activities — bias taxonomy worksheets, consequence scanning, the tarot cards of tech — these are qualitative tools for forming the hypothesis you then test. You cannot measure what you have not imagined. A pointer to resources is in the talk notes — there is a curated list at github.com/summerscope/mapping-fair-ml.'

the gap
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'These tools help us try to understand the state of the world — and that is valuable. But even if you successfully map what has happened historically, that is not the same as knowing how to intervene going forwards. What is the fair state you are actually working towards? Does fairness look like 50/50 men and non-men in tech? Equal representation of all genders and ethnicities in every profession relative to base population? These are genuinely hard questions that reasonable people disagree about. Simply running a preconfigured fairness metric across your system is a model-free approach to a question that demands a model. You are handing your moral responsibility to someone else's assumptions — which may or may not apply to your context, your domain, or your users.'

ethics is a practice, not a personality trait.
section five
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Before we talk about what to do, I want to name something that gets in the way of doing it. Shame. When people find out their system causes harm, the most common response is not curiosity — it is defensiveness. Because we have learned to treat ethical failure as a verdict on who we are, rather than information about what our system does. But ethics is not a personality trait. It is a practice. A capability you build over time. Finding harm in your system is evidence that you are looking — which is evidence that you are learning. Any system capable of causing no harm is also capable of doing nothing useful. The question is never whether your system is perfect — it isn't. It is whether you are taking the responsibility seriously.'
where can you intervene?
  1. problem statement — does the business model create incentives to find harm, or to ignore it?
  2. design ↔ build loop — what are you encoding before anyone uses it?
  3. build ↔ iterate loop — monitoring, feedback loops, learning from real use
  4. interventions — deliberate changes in response to what you've learned
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Intervention is available at every stage of building a system — and the earlier you intervene, the more radically different the impact on potential harms in the world. At the problem statement stage: does this business model create incentives to find harm, or to ignore it? If your revenue depends on engagement at any cost, monitoring for harm is structurally disincentivised. That is a design decision before a line of code is written. The design-build loop is where most assumptions get encoded invisibly. The build-iterate loop is where you discover what real people actually experience — through monitoring, feedback loops, qualitative signals. And interventions are deliberate course corrections made in response to what you have learned. Not the same as monitoring. Acting on the signal.'
design justice · Costanza-Chock, 2020

does the system need to know gender?

the scanner has a hard-coded gender binary. select female — flagged for chest area. select male — flagged for groin. the goal is to detect weapons.

Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'You do not need to know someone's gender to detect a weapon. That binary was encoded because someone assumed it was necessary. It was not. This is Sasha Costanza-Chock's example from Design Justice. But go further: what if instead of encoding the binary you offered the passenger a choice of differently-gendered officers? Airport security is famously performative. What if that performance made normative claims about how we could treat each other with respect and generosity, rather than suspicion and harm? What does real risk reduction actually look like when you start from the right question? This is what it means to intervene at the problem statement stage.'
codesign

design with, not for

  • bring people affected by the system into the design of it
  • not as test subjects — as co-designers with standing
  • "nothing about us without us" — disability rights movement, 1980s–90s
  • you will be bad at this at first. start anyway.
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Codesign is a set of practices for involving the people affected by a system in its design. The starting orientation is curiosity — genuinely wanting to understand the source of people's fears, reactions and needs, before deciding what to do about them. Without curiosity, says Kelly Ann McKercher in Beyond Sticky Notes, we won't spot anything new. We'll keep doing more of the same. Data scientists and engineers are used to being the technical authority in the room. Codesign requires becoming a beginner at something. The principle is borrowed from the disability rights movement: nothing about us without us. Design Justice extends it to every system that affects people who were not in the room when it was built. Resources: Beyond Sticky Notes by Kelly Ann McKercher — beyondstickynotes.com. The MoreThanCode report by the Tech for Social Justice Project. Arnstein's Ladder of Participation. All in the talk notes.'
design justice · Costanza-Chock, 2020

none of these options are me.

affordance — what a system enables you to do

dysaffordance — when a system forces you to misrepresent yourself to proceed

  • a job title dropdown that doesn't have your role
  • a nationality field that doesn't have your country
  • a gender field with two options
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'A dysaffordance is when a system forces you to misrepresent yourself in order to proceed. And I want to be clear: this is not only a problem for trans people, or for any particular marginalised group. We all experience dysaffordances constantly. Every time you have looked at a dropdown and thought "none of these are me" — that is one. A job title field that does not have your role. A nationality field that does not have your country. A form that assumes you have a middle name, or do not, or that your name fits in twenty characters. The question for system designers is not whether this affects marginalised groups. It is: which groups are we systematically forcing to misrepresent themselves, and what does that cost them over a lifetime of interactions? A minor inconvenience for one person is a daily accumulation of indignities for another. Universalist design — designing for everyone — makes these exclusions invisible rather than fixing them.'
consent-based intervention

offer. listen. adapt. stop when asked.

  • make the intervention visible — never act silently on someone's behalf
  • try once — if they say no, stop
  • if they say yes, learn — offer more things like it
  • never assume you know the answer next time
Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'I want to be precise about what I mean here — this is not about automation. It is about a learning relationship between the system and the individual. The system offers. The user decides. The system remembers. Two kinds of things a system might offer. First: interaction affordances. If someone is struggling with mouse clicks, the system notices and offers an alternative — keyboard shortcuts, voice input, a different pattern. Tries once. If yes, maybe offers more things like that. If no, stops and leaves them alone. Second: positive bias interventions. We think you may be disadvantaged by how this system works. Here is something we could do. Would you like it? The user decides. The system remembers the answer. Next time, it knows. Not a smarter algorithm making better guesses about what you need. A system that asks, listens, and defers to you.'

consent-based intervention

1

"yes, offer me more"

the system learns your preferences. surfaces relevant options. builds a picture of what help looks like for you specifically.

2

"no, leave me alone"

the system stops. does not try again. does not assume you'll change your mind. respects the preference as stated.

Laura Summers PyCon DE & PyData 2026
'Consider two students applying to the same college. Same gender, same ethnicity, same socioeconomic background on paper. The system flags both as candidates for positive intervention. One student is grateful — they want every advantage they can get. The other student explicitly does not want their background factored in. They want to feel they are competing on a level playing field. Both preferences are valid. Both should be honoured. Now — and this is the important part — we know the biases are already baked in. Making a positive intervention probably does get closer to a level playing field than doing nothing. But that is our belief, not theirs. And we should not impose our belief about what fairness looks like on people whose experience of fairness is theirs to define. This applies whether you are offering a different interaction pattern, inviting someone to flag disadvantage, or adjusting how your system weights their application. Ask. Listen. Remember. Never assume.'
