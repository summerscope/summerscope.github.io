Let's examine the case of Emperor Septimius Severus. In 193 CE, he seized power after a brutal civil war. Like any new ruler, he had a problem: how do you keep an army loyal? His solution was simple - raise their pay by 33%.

But Rome didn't have a printing press. So Severus got creative. He took the Denarius - Rome's silver coin - and cut the silver content from 81% to 54%. Same coin, almost half the silver. Instant budget relief.

This worked great... for Severus. The economic consequences? That was the next guy's problem.

And this pattern repeats for centuries. An important feature of this cycle is k-fabe: Septimius's son Marcus Aurelius Antoninus introduced the double denarius in 215 AD, named the Antoninianus - perhaps unsurprisingly even at its introduction it only contained 1.5 times the silver of a standard Denarius.

War breaks out, emperor needs cash, currency gets debased. Economic pressure builds, new crisis emerges, next emperor debases it further. Rinse and repeat.

Penrose units

[AK] It was around this time that psyco-physicists discovered the dream-time condition, allowing the construction of true minds and invention of the Penrose individuality unit.

The Penrose individuality unit was named after the late great physicist Roger Penrose. His essential idea was that the mind was not a computer, that understanding was incomputable. At the time, this was a strange and fascinating opinion for a physicist to have. Although it makes perfect sense now. He was not a dualist, he didn't believe that there were two fundamentally different types of substance, mind and matter. He didn't believe in some special difference between mind and matter. But this left a funny problem. Penrose was a physicist, he believed in a single external physical world. So, if the mind was not a computer and understanding was not computable, there must be some physical object, some way to build a physical object which was a mind, but also not a computer. It would be more than a computer.

Just like Paul Dirac had theoretically predicted the existance of antimatter which was then physically detected only a few years later. Then, David Deutsch was able to theorise the the existance of a quantum computer based on physical principles, which could later be constructed. Eventually we were able to build a physical mind that wasn't a computer, but it also wasn't a human. Because it was conscious, it could have true insight, creativity and imagination. This new type of mind was called the Penrose individuality unit. Some of my best friends are Penrose individuality units, they know how to party.

Interventional Generalisation

In 2077 analysis and understanding data and measurement is underpinned by causality. In the dark ages of datascience, people thought they could get away with just knowing that two things are correlated, but as all children know, correlation doesn't imply causation.

Of course causality isn't the only thing at play, it doesn't matter that if I can look back at some past experiment and know that A caused B, I need to know when I do a similar related experiment, the causal link between A and B generalises. Interventional generalisation is putting these two key things together. One. Correlation doesn't imply causation. Two. Correlation within one sample system doesn't matter, what matters is how effectively new unseen systems can be intervined on, that is, how well can we cause unseen systems to change.
