The gap between the metric and the harm

Vocabulary time. Two categories of harm. Both matter. Not the same thing.

Harms of allocation

when a system withholds or unfairly distributes opportunities, resources or penalties.

Harms of representation

when a system reinforces the subordination of a group through how it depicts or describes people.

Barocas, Crawford, Shapiro & Wallach, 2017 · Crawford, NeurIPS 2017

Crawford, NeurIPS 2017 keynote "The Trouble with Bias" + Barocas/Crawford/Shapiro/Wallach 2017. Allocation = what you get or don't (loans, jobs, sentences). Representation = how people are seen or erased. Representation sits at the root of allocation harms downstream.

Harms of allocation

Low risk

an automated rewards system at a doctor's surgery. algorithm de-prioritises the treat for patients outside the target age range. a child doesn't get a candy. upsetting but recoverable.

High risk

COMPAS. twice as likely to falsely flag Black defendants as future offenders. people stayed in prison longer based on a score nobody could explain. the time does not come back. (Angwin et al., ProPublica 2016)

Low: child doesn't get a candy. Recoverable. High: COMPAS — US courts, recidivism prediction. 2x false positive rate for Black defendants (ProPublica 2016). Judges deferred. Time doesn't come back. Same habits of mind at different stakes.

Harms of representation

Low risk

a face filter that puts bunny ears on you. cute if your face is detected. the effect breaks for darker skin tones. annoying. a minor indignity. nobody's life is at stake.

High risk

a teenage girl searches for mathematicians and sees almost entirely white men. she updates her sense of what's possible. she changes course. the erasure compounds.

Low: face filter breaks on darker skin. "We didn't think about you." High: girl searches for mathematicians, sees white men, updates her sense of what's possible. Hidden Figures problem — erasure compounds across generations. Representational harm reproduces inequality, not just reflects it.

An unreliable narrator judging its own reliability
The gap
Three eval modes: QA (gating deployment), observability (annotation of query-response pairs), guardrails (LLM judges in production). You can write custom fairness evals — but you're relying on the system you're evaluating. Same blind spots that produced the output are evaluating it. Can't proofread your own work.

The four jobs

  • A Understand the world

  • B Understand foundation models

  • C Understand your system

  • D Intervene

A/B/C: build intuition then measure — world, foundation models, your system. Nested: system inside models inside world. Most people only do C, usually post-production. A and B almost always skipped. D: intervention — where understanding meets action. Without intersectionality you systematically miss the most marginalised.

Section three

What genuine intervention looks like

This is a skill and a discipline, not a personality trait. Any system capable of doing no harm is also not doing much of anything. I encourage you to try and to give yourself grace and patience when you fail.
Design justice

Community-led practices to build the worlds we need

Sasha Costanza-Chock (they/them), MIT Press, 2020. Freely available online.

Core argument: power in design — who has it, what they do with it, effects on oppressed groups. Grapples with how universalist design principles erase intersectionally disadvantaged people.
Design justice network

designjustice.org

founded 2015 at the Allied Media Conference. 10 principles centring people normally marginalised by design. available in multiple languages.

principle #2 We center the voices of those who are directly impacted by the outcomes of the design process.

principle #5 We see the role of the designer (data scientist) as a facilitator rather than an expert.

principle #6 We believe that everyone is an expert based on their own lived experience, and that we all have unique and brilliant contributions to bring to a design process.

10 principles — commitments, not slogans. Read all ten at designjustice.org.

Qualitative + quantitative

A metric is only as good as the hypothesis behind it

  • study the domain — read, follow your curiosity
  • talk to people affected by the system
  • use scanning tools and design activities
  • then measure what you've learned to look for
Quant and qual are complements. Library doesn't tell you what to look for — domain knowledge, literature, talking to affected people does. Bias taxonomy worksheets, consequence scanning, tarot cards of tech. Can't measure what you haven't imagined. Resources: github.com/summerscope/mapping-fair-ml.
Case study

"Let's make it fair."

  • two colleagues. one concern. one meeting. everyone leaves satisfied.
  • the ML engineer goes off to tune the model.
  • the product lead goes off to watch the dashboard.
  • neither checks what the other means by "fair."
CV screener. Group A: traditional CS degrees. Group B: bootcamp/self-taught. Model trained on historical hiring data — credentialist. Two colleagues agree "make it fair." No further discussion. Neither defines fair.

Six months later

The engineer

tuned the model to achieve equalized odds: the true positive rate and false positive rate are equal across both groups.

"the model works equally well for both groups. it's fair."

The product lead

checked the dashboard. Group A candidates: 52% advanced. Group B candidates: 35% advanced.

"this isn't fair."

Both right. Engineer optimised for equalized odds (equal accuracy). Product lead expected demographic parity (equal outcomes). Different questions.
choosing a metric is choosing a value system.
The point
Three questions the metric can't answer: (1) does base rate difference reflect capability or historical disadvantage? (2) which error is worse? (3) what world are you trying to build?
installing a library is not a moral position. it is the absence of one.
Fairness washing
Mapping history ≠ knowing how to intervene. What's the fair state you're working towards? 50/50? Population-proportional? Hard questions. Preconfigured metric = handing your moral responsibility to someone else's assumptions. LLM eval frameworks have narrowed ambition — from demographic equity to "contains slurs?" We've regressed.

Codesign

Design with, not for

  • bring people affected by the system into the design of it
  • not as test subjects — as co-designers with standing
  • "nothing about us without us" — disability rights movement, 1980s–90s
  • you will be bad at this at first. start anyway.
Starting orientation: curiosity. McKercher (Beyond Sticky Notes): without curiosity we won't spot anything new. Engineers are used to being the authority — codesign requires becoming a beginner. Resources: beyondstickynotes.com, MoreThanCode report, Arnstein's Ladder.
Consent-based intervention

Offer. Listen. Adapt. Stop when asked.

  • make the intervention visible — never act silently on someone's behalf
  • try once — if they say no, stop
  • if they say yes, learn — offer more things like it
  • never assume you know the answer next time
Not automation — a learning relationship. System offers, user decides, system remembers. Two kinds: (1) interaction affordances — struggling with mouse clicks? Offer keyboard shortcuts. (2) positive bias interventions — "we think you may be disadvantaged, here's something we could do, want it?" Not smarter guessing. Asking and deferring.
Consent-based intervention
1

"Yes, offer me more"

the system learns your preferences. surfaces relevant options. builds a picture of what help looks like for you specifically.

2

"No, leave me alone"

the system stops. does not try again. does not assume you'll change your mind. respects the preference as stated.

Two students, same demographics on paper. One wants every advantage. The other doesn't want background factored in. Both valid. We know biases are baked in — intervention probably helps. But that's our belief, not theirs. Don't impose your model of fairness on people whose experience is theirs to define.
«
Do you identify as a member of a disadvantaged group? If yes, we'd like to adjust how this system works in your favour. Would you like us to do that?
»
Uncomfortable to say out loud — that's appropriate. Don't resolve the discomfort by deciding quietly on someone's behalf. Goal: treat people as individuals with agency. No easy answers. Better questions.
